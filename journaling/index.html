Learning to Fall in Love with Journaling

Nicholas Clay February 17, 2026

A couple of weeks ago I ordered a hobonichi cousin planner. Now while I had wanted one, and had been following users of the community closely since the middle of 2025, the planner itself remained rotting away in my Amazon cart: unpurchased. Then 2026 rolled around and I was nearing a breaking point with my relationship toward digital journaling. There was an innate friction I had everytime I tried to write, no matter how much I attempted to persevere through the discomfort. It honestly felt like I was in an abusive relationship with the only healthy solution being walking away completely from my abuser - the noxious glaring screen. My eyes in recent years have become deeply annoyed with excessive screen usage: a byproduct of dimwitted, decade-long exposure since childhood. Focusing onto a screen that I had already been on for most of the day made journaling less motivating to say the least. I had gotten to the point where I actually dispised writing at all. My goal in 2026 was to consciously reduce the screentime my eyes were in front of - both for my poor vision and my mental health. I will always stand by the fact that some words are better suited with good ole' pen and paper, and I think this experiment with virtual journaling made that point abundently clear to me. So from that, and without much hesitation now, I clicked the "add to checkout" button. Within a mere days later - like magic, I held in my hands the very planner that I had heard so much about. Looking back to that moment in hindsight, I had no idea what it would unlock for me in the future. Something I had been missing in my life dearly.

If you are familiar with the hobonichi planner ecosystem it can be very overwhelming at first sight. While there is guidelines of how you can use the planner out of the box the real adventure is making it your own. The planner can be a novel into your entire year, while simulataneously keeping tabs of the most important events in a traditional calendar-like format. That is enough creative freedom to get anyone to go down an infinite rabbit hole of curating the perfect writing and organizational enviroment. But I beg you not to fall into this trap... because I've done this very thing with planners in the past and was starting to do it again. The only way to combat the blank slate is to just take a leap of faith and go. Let me make it clear, there are so many great creators on the internet who will show you what they do with their hobonichi planners. While creative inspiration from others is a great virtue to adopt, the journey that you take by being spontaneous and just writing without a clear gameplan will reward you with something completely original. That originiality is a blessing in today's world. So harness it, and chances are you will probably fall in love with the process of journaling far more than you think.

I wrote my first journal on February 9th 2026 under the large daily section which sat nested conviently behind the monthly and weekly planners. Now this section could be used for anything - because this is a hobonichi planner after all, but personally it was the ideal free space to keep a daily journal for myself. I could add stickers, pictures, and even drawings for each entry while still having the rest of the page to write down all the things I needed to say. But that was the issue, What do I say? This wasn't a new painpoint for me and was quite frankly persistent in all my attempts previous. When I was trying to keep a digital log running in the years prior I could never get on top of this problem. A irritatation that plagued my ability to take things from my brain and manifest them into flowing sentences on a page. I knew I had a lot to say, but I lacked the direction that I wanted to take with my writing. I would go back and forth thinking I had something to talk about only to realize I could get down maybe a sentence or two and like clockwork I would stumble back to the beginning. This never-ending mental struggle continued on repeat over and over again like a broken record. I know that I am not the only one to struggle with this problem, and unfortunately I believe our issues collectively stem from us being taught to write and not how to approach thinking about writing itself. In other words, its not about what to write but how to write.

I'll give myself some grace here, but the solution to my problem was something so simple and right in front of my face the whole time. I think as a society we overcomplicate things all the time because we are natural problem-solvers with a powerful built in computer that likes to stay busy all the time. I searched far and wide for a solution to my writers block to no avail, until I stumbled upon the renound Japanese novelist Haruki Murakami. If I had to describe Marukami's writing philosophy loosely its: using the mundane aspects of life as send off points into avenues of deeper thoughts. Say you take a second to write about a coffee you had made in the early morning of that same day. On the surface you would probably say: Nicholas that sounds like the most boring piece of writing you could come up with. I make the same damn cup of joe every morning, what is there to write about? However, I would say that "boring" thing that you do everyday has more to say about you then you probably think. Those things that say the most about you are the exact things that will unlock your inner creativity. How would you describe that very moment of making the cup of coffee? Was the world still dark outside when you made it? What was the aroma like when you opened up the sealed coffee bag? What did the coffee taste like that morning? Do you put cream and sugar in your coffee or are you a straight black coffee drinker? What was the sensation like when you took that the first sip? Is there a feeling you get everytime you take that first sip? As you begin to dive deep into analyzing this mundane aspect of your day to day life you begin to unlock a plethora of things to talk about. There is something liberating when you approach writing like this. Now obviously this is just a blueprint, but I highly recommend checking out his book What I talk about when I talk about running. There you will see what I am alluding to here. I think his description of running as beautifully streneous lead me to download the book on my new pocket e-ink reader. In fact he says running itself helped him become a better fiction writer - pretty neat if you think about it. If you need another reason to get the book, I linked a youtube video that, in addition to researching Murakami, helped inspire me to pick it up too. I never try to force myself to write, and I think that is where I've found the most freedom to continue writing. Even if I can't write daily journals I will backlog my ideas with sticky notes on the pages and come back to fill in the blanks at a later time. Sometimes it can take me days to ponder what had happened and that is okay. Opening the pages and seeing my backlog notes on the day reminds me, "oh yeah let me get to that". One of my biggest gripes to digital journaling was having to purse through files on files in my obsidian vault with the expectation that I would come back and fill it in. There was so much friction between me and writing that the likelihood I would comeback and finish a specific entry at a later date was far and few. Because I take my planner with me everywhere, whether I am in my car, or in a coffee shop I can just open it up and start writing. Why change a basic system that works? Digital Journaling attempts to make our thoughts accessible whenever and whereever we are in the cloud, but I'd argue that it has killed our relationship with one of our most primitive and important relationships: Our relationship to physically create words in the physical world.

Ultimately, I think the reason I love writing in my planner is because it feels like the only pure thing to exist today. In modern times, where disillusion is rampant, my hobonichi stands in a camp of candorness. It grounds me and makes me appreciate the little moments I had in my day. It gives me the space to ponder the thoughts I had, and even the conversations with others in that day. The archieved collection of my writings acts as a marker in time that I can always go back to and revist. Where I didn't have an outlet before, and felt like I was yelling into a void, I now have my planner to listen to what I had to say. As I have gotten older I've come to realise that truth is arbitrary. We learn, grow, and materialize our own ideas over time. We stumble over and over again making mistakes as we go along life's journey. Your journal gets to be an insight into that because that is what it is really: It is your story.
